Accommodation of the Eye
Definition
The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length to focus on objects at different distances, by changing its curvature using ciliary muscles.
👁 Near Object (<25 cm)
  • Ciliary muscles contract
  • Lens becomes thicker (more curved)
  • Focal length decreases
  • Greater converging power
🔭 Far Object (>25 cm)
  • Ciliary muscles relax
  • Lens becomes thinner (less curved)
  • Focal length increases
  • Less converging power needed
Range of Accommodation
Minimum object distance (Near Point)25 cm (normal adult)
Maximum object distance (Far Point)Infinity (normal eye)
Presbyopia (age-related loss of accommodation)
Ciliary muscles weaken with age. The lens loses flexibility. Both near and far vision may be affected. Corrected with bifocal lenses.
Myopia (Short-sightedness)
Defect
  • Cannot see distant objects clearly
  • Image forms in front of retina
  • Far point moves closer than infinity
Causes
  • Eyeball too long (front-to-back)
  • Lens too converging (thick)
  • Cornea too curved
✓ Correction
Concave (diverging) lens — negative power
  • Concave lens diverges rays before they enter the eye
  • Eye's lens then converges them onto the retina
  • Far point of myopic eye acts as virtual object
Lens power = −1/d
d = far point distance (in metres)
Example
A person can see clearly up to 2 m (far point). Concave lens needed:
P = −1/2 = −0.5 D (focal length = −2 m)
Hypermetropia (Far-sightedness)
Defect
  • Cannot see near objects clearly
  • Image forms behind the retina
  • Near point is farther than 25 cm
Causes
  • Eyeball too short
  • Lens too weak (flat)
  • Cornea less curved
✓ Correction
Convex (converging) lens — positive power
  • Convex lens converges rays additionally
  • Eye's lens can then bring image onto retina
  • Convex lens forms virtual image at the near point
P = 1/f  (positive power)
Convex lens with appropriate focal length

All corrections work by ensuring the final image falls exactly on the retina. The corrective lens compensates for the eye's inability to focus.

Refraction Through a Glass Slab
Key Result
The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced (shifted sideways).
Why?
  • At entry (air→glass): bends toward normal
  • At exit (glass→air): bends away from normal
  • Both refractions cancel direction change
  • But net lateral shift remains
Lateral Displacement Factors
  • Increases with slab thickness
  • Increases with angle of incidence
  • Increases with higher refractive index
  • Zero for normal incidence (∠i = 0)
Application
This is why a pen in water appears shifted when viewed from the side. Also why objects seen through thick glass appear displaced.
Atmospheric Refraction
What is it?
Refraction of light as it passes through Earth's atmosphere, which has gradually varying density (and hence refractive index) with altitude.
Key Effects
  • Advance Sunrise — Sun visible ~2 min before actual sunrise
  • Delayed Sunset — Sun visible ~2 min after actual sunset
  • Extra daylight — ~4 extra minutes total per day
  • Sun appears oval at horizon — vertical refraction more than horizontal
Twinkling of Stars
  • Stars are point sources far away
  • Atmospheric turbulence constantly changes refractive index
  • Apparent position of star shifts rapidly
  • This rapid flickering = twinkling
  • Planets do NOT twinkle — extended disc, shifts average out
Remember
Light from the Sun (or stars) travels from rarer medium (space) to denser medium (lower atmosphere), bending toward Earth's surface. The apparent position is always higher than the actual position.
Dispersion of Light
Definition
Splitting of white light into its component colours by a prism, due to different wavelengths having different refractive indices in glass.
Violet Indigo Blue Green Yellow Orange Red
VIBGYOR
Violet bends most ↑ | Red bends least ↓
Why Different Bending?
Shorter wavelength → higher refractive index in glass → slower speed → more bending.

Violet (λ ≈ 400 nm): n ≈ 1.53
Red (λ ≈ 700 nm): n ≈ 1.51

The difference causes the colours to diverge.
Recombination
A second inverted prism can recombine the VIBGYOR spectrum back into white light — proving white light is a mixture of all colours.
Rainbow — Natural Dispersion
Formation Steps
  • Sunlight enters a water droplet
  • Refraction at entry — dispersion starts
  • Total Internal Reflection at back wall
  • Refraction again at exit — dispersion increases
  • Different colours exit at different angles
  • Red: 42°  |  Violet: 40° (from Sun-observer line)
Red: outer arc (42°)
Violet: inner arc (40°)
Conditions to See Rainbow
  • Sun must be behind the observer
  • Rain/water droplets in front
  • Usually seen in morning (facing west) or afternoon (facing east)
Double Rainbow
A secondary (fainter) rainbow forms outside the primary one with colours reversed — light undergoes two internal reflections inside the droplet.
Each droplet sends only one colour to the observer's eye at a given angle. Millions of droplets together produce the full arc.
Scattering of Light
Rayleigh Scattering Law
I ∝ 1/λ⁴
Intensity of scattered light ∝ 1/(wavelength)⁴
Shorter wavelength → scattered much more intensely. Blue (λ≈450 nm) scattered ~5.8× more than red (λ≈700 nm).
💙 Blue Sky
Sunlight passes through atmosphere. Blue light scatters in all directions from gas molecules. We see scattered blue from all directions → sky looks blue.
🌅 Red Sunset
Near horizon, sunlight travels longer path. Blue, violet, green scatter away. Only red and orange (long λ, least scattered) reach observer → red/orange sky.
🔦 Tyndall Effect
Colloidal particles scatter visible light. A beam of light becomes visible in smoke, fog, or milk (colloids). True solutions do not show this.
Examples of Tyndall Effect
Light beam in dusty room, car headlights in fog, light through glass of milk, blue colour of ice seen from certain angles.
Explaining Common Phenomena
💙 Why is the sky blue?
Air molecules preferentially scatter shorter wavelengths (blue). The scattered blue light fills the sky from all directions. Near the horizon, more atmosphere is traversed → less blue remains.
🌅 Why are sunsets red?
At sunrise/sunset, light travels through maximum atmosphere length. Blue, violet, green are scattered away. Red/orange (scattered least) dominate → red sky.
⭐ Why do stars twinkle?
Stars are distant point sources. Atmospheric turbulence shifts their apparent position rapidly → apparent brightness fluctuates → twinkling.
🪐 Why don't planets twinkle?
Planets are closer — they appear as discs, not points. Random shifts from many parts of the disc average out → stable apparent brightness → no twinkling.
Quick Memory Aid
Blue sky = scattering nearby  |  Red sunset = scattering far away  |  Twinkling = atmospheric turbulence  |  Rainbow = dispersion + TIR in droplets
Vision Correction — Power of Lens
P = 1/f (metres)
Dioptre (D)
Myopia (concave)P < 0 (negative)
Hypermetropia (convex)P > 0 (positive)
Worked Examples
Myopia: Far point = 50 cm
P = −1/0.5 = −2 D

Hypermetropia: Near point = 1 m (should be 25 cm)
Lens must form virtual image at 1 m for object at 25 cm
1/f = 1/(−1) − 1/(−0.25) = −1 + 4 = 3
P = +3 D
Optometrist Prescription
Lenses are prescribed in Dioptres. A positive prescription corrects hypermetropia; negative corrects myopia. Bifocals have two regions of different power.

Two-Prism Experiment
  • Prism 1 disperses white light → VIBGYOR
  • Prism 2 (inverted) recombines → white light
  • Proves white light = all colours mixed
Newton's Experiment
Newton first showed in 1666 that white light from the sun is a mixture of all colours. He used a second prism to recombine the spectrum back into white light.
Angle of Deviation
Violet deviates most → highest refractive index in glass for violet light. Angular dispersion = deviation of violet − deviation of red.
