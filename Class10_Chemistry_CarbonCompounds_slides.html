Carbon and Its Compounds
Class 10 Science | NCERT | Karnataka KTBS Syllabus
Carbon forms millions of compounds — more than any other element!
Electronic config: 2, 4 · Atomic number: 6 · Valency: 4
Carbon in Daily Life
Carbon is everywhere around us
🍚 Food
Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), proteins, fats — all contain carbon. Every living cell is carbon-based.
⛽ Fuel
LPG (C₃H₈/C₄H₁₀), petrol, diesel, coal, natural gas — all carbon compounds.
👕 Clothes & Plastics
Nylon, polyester, rubber — synthetic fibres and plastics are carbon compounds.
💎 Diamond & ✏️ Graphite
Pure forms of carbon! Same element, completely different properties.
💊 Medicines
Aspirin, penicillin, paracetamol — all organic (carbon-based) molecules.
🌿 Life Itself
DNA, proteins, cell walls — all made of carbon. Life = carbon chemistry.
📌 Organic Chemistry = the study of carbon compounds (originally thought to come only from living organisms)
Why Carbon is Special
Two unique properties make millions of compounds possible
1️⃣ Tetravalency
Carbon has 4 electrons in its outer shell. It needs 4 more to complete the shell.

Instead of losing or gaining 4 electrons (needs too much energy), carbon shares its electrons — forming covalent bonds.

This means carbon can form 4 bonds with other atoms.
2️⃣ Catenation
Carbon atoms can bond with each other to form long chains, branched chains, and rings.

No other element shows this property to such a large extent. This is why millions of carbon compounds exist!
Covalent Bond — Sharing Electrons
Carbon shares electrons instead of gaining or losing them
A covalent bond forms when two atoms share a pair of electrons to complete their outer shells.

Three types of covalent bonds:

Single Bond (—)
Share 1 pair of electrons · Example: C-H in methane
H—C (C shares 1 e⁻, H shares 1 e⁻)
Double Bond (=)
Share 2 pairs of electrons · Example: C=C in ethene
C=C (each C shares 2 e⁻)
Triple Bond (≡)
Share 3 pairs of electrons · Example: C≡C in ethyne
C≡C (each C shares 3 e⁻)
Allotropes of Carbon
Same element — completely different properties!
📌 Allotrope: Different physical forms of the same element with different arrangements of atoms.
💎 Diamond

3D tetrahedral network

✅ Transparent
❌ Bad electrical conductor

Uses: Cutting tools, jewellery, drill bits

Hydrocarbons — Introduction
Compounds containing only Carbon and Hydrogen
A hydrocarbon contains only Carbon (C) and Hydrogen (H). They are the simplest organic compounds.
Saturated Hydrocarbons
All single bonds (C–C) only
Called Saturated — no more H can be added

General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
Family name: Alkanes

Example: Methane CH₄, Ethane C₂H₆
Unsaturated Hydrocarbons
Contains double bonds (C=C) or triple bonds (C≡C)
Called Unsaturated — more H can be added

Double bond family: Alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ)
Triple bond family: Alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂)

Example: Ethene C₂H₄, Ethyne C₂H₂
🔁 Cyclic (Ring) Compounds
Carbon chains can close back on themselves to form rings.
Example: Benzene (C₆H₆) — 6 carbons in a hexagonal ring, alternating single and double bonds.
Cyclic hydrocarbons can be saturated (cyclohexane) or unsaturated (benzene).
C₆H₆ — benzene ring (arene family)
💡 Unsaturated compounds are more reactive than saturated because the double/triple bonds are easier to break.

Homologous Series
A family of carbon compounds with the same functional group
Homologous series: a series of organic compounds that have the same functional group, same general formula, and differ from each other by a –CH₂– unit.

4 Key Properties of a Homologous Series:

1. Same General Formula
All alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
All alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ
2. Similar Chemical Properties
Same functional group → same type of reactions
Methane and butane both combust the same way
3. Gradually Changing Physical Properties
Boiling point, melting point, density increase as chain length increases
4. Each Member Differs by CH₂
Methane (CH₄) → Ethane (C₂H₆) → Propane (C₃H₈)
Each adds 14 mass units

Naming Carbon Compounds — IUPAC Rules
International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry naming system

Steps to Name:

  1. Find longest carbon chain → gives parent name
    1C=meth, 2C=eth, 3C=prop, 4C=but, 5C=pent
  2. Number carbons from the end nearest to functional group
  3. Add suffix for functional group
    -ane (alkane), -ene (alkene), -ol (alcohol), -oic acid
  4. Name side chains with their position number

Examples:

CH₃–CH₂–OH
2 carbons (eth) + OH (alcohol = -ol)
→ Ethanol
CH₃–COOH
2 carbons (eth) + COOH (-oic acid)
→ Ethanoic acid
CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃
Longest chain: 3C (propane) + CH₃ branch at C2
→ 2-methylpropane
Chemical Properties — Combustion
Carbon compounds are excellent fuels
Complete Combustion
Excess oxygen, clean burn
Blue flame, no smoke

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Products: CO₂ + H₂O + heat
Incomplete Combustion
Limited oxygen supply
Yellow/sooty flame

2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O
Products: CO + soot (carbon)
CO is toxic gas! 🚨
⚠️ Always ensure good ventilation when burning carbon fuels — CO poisoning is dangerous!

Soaps and Detergents
How are soaps made? What makes them clean?

Making Soap — Saponification

Fat/Oil + NaOH (or KOH) → Soap + Glycerol
Fat + NaOH →(heat)→ Soap + Glycerol
  • NaOH makes hard soap (bar soap)
  • KOH makes soft soap (liquid soap, shaving cream)

Soap Molecule Structure:

Long hydrophobic tail: –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–... (non-polar, repels water, loves grease)

Ionic hydrophilic head: –COO⁻Na⁺ (polar, loves water, repels grease)
How Soap Cleans — Micelle Formation
The science of washing dirt away

Step-by-step:

  1. Soap is added to dirty (greasy) water
  2. Hydrophobic tails of soap molecules point toward the grease (like dissolves like)
  3. Hydrophilic heads face outward toward water
  4. A micelle forms — a spherical cluster with grease trapped inside
  5. Micelle is soluble in water (outer surface is ionic/polar)
  6. Micelle washes away with water, taking the grease with it! ✅
⚠️ Soap does NOT work in hard water — it forms a white scum (insoluble calcium/magnesium salts). Use detergents instead!

Key Points:

  • Carbon's unique properties: tetravalency + catenation
  • Allotropes: Diamond (hardest, insulator), Graphite (soft, conductor), Fullerene (C₆₀)
  • Alkanes = saturated (single bonds), Alkenes = C=C, Alkynes = C≡C
  • Ethanol: antiseptic, solvent, fuel; methanol is POISONOUS

  • Soap works by micelle formation
  • Soap fails in hard water → use detergent
  • Test for unsaturation: decolourizes bromine water
